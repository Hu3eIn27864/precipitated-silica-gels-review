## PSG-027 — Bałdyga et al. (2012), wet-process precipitation and aggregation

**Reference:** J. Bałdyga, M. Jasińska, K. Jodko and P. Petelski, “Precipitation of amorphous colloidal silica from aqueous solutions—Aggregation problem”, *Chemical Engineering Science* 77 (2012), 207–216. DOI: 10.1016/j.ces.2012.03.046.

**Verification:** Publisher record, abstract and detailed publisher preview inspected; author list cross-checked against an independent bibliographic record. Full article not inspected.

**System / methods:** Batch precipitation of amorphous silica by neutralising dilute sodium silicate with sulfuric acid in a baffled stirred tank (tank diameter 145 mm). Standard impeller speed was 200 rpm. Effects of pH, temperature, silica concentration and NaCl concentration were investigated. Particle-size evolution was measured with a Malvern Zetasizer ZS ZEN 3500. Aggregation was interpreted using DLVO-based colloidal stability calculations, surface composition and surface-reaction hypotheses.

**Directly reported observations:** The study reports particle formation and aggregation over pH 0.85–10.5. The smallest particles remain a few nanometres in size across the pH range, while their formation rate increases strongly with pH. The abstract concludes that attractive and repulsive colloidal forces alone do not explain the observed aggregation; surface reactions also need to be considered.

**Supports:** This is independent experimental evidence in the sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid wet-process family that pH, electrolyte and solution conditions affect particle formation and aggregation. It supports a coupled picture in which colloidal forces and surface chemistry both matter, rather than a DLVO-only account.

**Does not establish:** It does not study the industrial semi-batch concentration/feed history in the Schlomach–Quarch work, nor post-gel fragmentation, wet-fragment consolidation or syneresis. Its dilute batch regime should not be conflated with concentrated semi-batch production.

**Limitations:** DLS-derived size distributions are method- and model-dependent, particularly for polydisperse, evolving aggregates. The surface composition and modified stability ratio are interpreted quantities, not direct measurements of every interparticle force or reaction rate. Full methods and figures should be checked before extracting quantitative trends.

**Review role:** Strong independent comparator for wet-process silica nucleation/aggregation and the need to include surface reactions in mechanistic models. **Class A for precipitation/aggregation in its own batch system; not direct evidence for syneresis. OBS+CALC+MODEL.**

## PSG-028 — Tognonvi et al. (2011), irreversible gel formation and syneresis in basic medium

**Reference:** M. T. Tognonvi, S. Rossignol and J.-P. Bonnet, “Effect of alkali cation on irreversible gel formation in basic medium”, *Journal of Non-Crystalline Solids* 357(1) (2011), 43–49. DOI: 10.1016/j.jnoncrysol.2010.10.003.

**Verification:** Publisher abstract and DOI metadata inspected; full article not inspected.

**System / methods:** Commercial lithium, sodium and potassium silicate solutions in basic medium; gels formed by acidifying alkaline silicate solutions with hydrochloric acid. Alkali-cation identity is the central variable.

**Directly reported observation:** The publisher abstract explicitly reports a syneresis phenomenon during gel ripening that leads to formation of a strongly consolidated solid, and frames the work around irreversible gel formation and consolidation.

**Supports:** Syneresis and irreversible consolidation are not unique to the sulfuric-acid industrial precipitated-silica studies. Related sodium-silicate gel systems show ripening-associated liquid expulsion and strengthening, with alkali-cation chemistry as a potentially important variable.

**Does not establish:** The result cannot be transferred quantitatively to acid-precipitated industrial silica fragments. The HCl-acidified basic silicate route, cation composition and gel chemistry differ from the highly acidic sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid route.

**Limitations:** Full experimental methods, syneresis measurement protocol, mechanical-strength measurement and the relative contributions of physical and chemical bonding remain to be inspected.

**Review role:** Independent mechanistic comparator for silicate-gel ripening, syneresis and irreversible consolidation. **Class B; OBS, with mechanism pending full-text appraisal.**

## PSG-029 — Tognonvi et al. (2019), chemical species during basic sodium-silicate gel ripening

**Reference:** M. T. Tognonvi, A. Lecomte, S. Rossignol and J.-P. Bonnet, “Ripening of Na-silicate gels in basic media: Identification of involved chemical species”, *Journal of Non-Crystalline Solids* 522 (2019), 119482. DOI: 10.1016/j.jnoncrysol.2019.119482.

**Verification:** Publisher metadata and highlights inspected; full article not inspected.

**System / methods:** Basic sodium-silicate gels during ripening/maturation, with focus on the chemical species involved in gelation and ageing.

**Reported highlights:** The publisher describes Si7-type colloids as relevant to stabilisation of concentrated sodium silicate; distinguishes reversible gels controlled by physical interactions (including van der Waals interactions) from stable silica compounds obtained through dissolution/precipitation reactions.

**Supports:** Silicate-gel maturation can involve different regimes: reversible physical association and more stable chemical evolution through dissolution/precipitation. This is useful for framing a coupled physical/chemical mechanism rather than assuming a single universal bonding process.

**Does not establish:** The publisher highlights do not establish that the same species or transition govern industrial acid-precipitated silica gel fragments. They do not by themselves quantify the contribution of each process to macroscopic syneresis.

**Limitations:** Full text is required to inspect speciation assignments, analytical methods, pH/time trajectories and evidence for the proposed reversible-to-stable transition. Do not cite highlights as direct proof of a specific molecular pathway in the target material.

**Review role:** Mechanistic comparator for basic sodium-silicate gel ripening and dissolution/precipitation. **Class B; OBS+CALC with mechanistic interpretation HYP pending full-text appraisal.**

## PSG-024 update — Shapiro & Kolthoff (1950), thermal ageing of precipitated silica

The abstract text reproduced in an accessible bibliographic page reports that thermal ageing was assessed through changes in specific surface area (water and dye adsorption) and apparent density (mercury pycnometry). The reported interpretation is that below approximately 700 °C, surface diffusion/perfection of primary particles decreases surface area with little porosity loss; above approximately 700 °C, bulk diffusion/sintering produces a marked reduction in both porosity and surface area.

**Important qualification:** This summary was available through a secondary article page reproducing the abstract; the ACS full text was not accessible. Treat it as abstract-level evidence, not a full-text critical appraisal. It concerns thermal treatment of precipitated silica powder/gel at high temperature, not aqueous syneresis.

## Synthesis update

The independent evidence base now supports three distinct points more strongly:

1. **Wet-process precipitation/aggregation:** Bałdyga et al. (2012) independently study sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid precipitation and find that colloidal forces alone are insufficient to account for aggregation; surface reactions must also be considered.
2. **Syneresis outside the industrial acid route:** Tognonvi et al. (2011) explicitly report ripening-associated syneresis and consolidation in basic alkali-silicate gels.
3. **Chemical maturation:** Tognonvi et al. (2019) distinguish reversible physical gels from stable silica compounds associated with dissolution/precipitation in basic sodium-silicate systems.

These papers materially improve mechanistic breadth but do **not** remove the principal gap: independently replicated quantitative syneresis measurements on industrially relevant, acid-precipitated silica fragments remain scarce.
