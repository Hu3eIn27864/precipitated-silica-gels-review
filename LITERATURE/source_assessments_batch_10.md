## PSG-032 — Hamouda & Akhlaghi Amiri (2014), alkaline sodium-silicate gelation and syneresis

**Reference:** A. A. Hamouda and H. A. Akhlaghi Amiri, “Factors Affecting Alkaline Sodium Silicate Gelation for In-Depth Reservoir Profile Modification,” *Energies* 7(2) (2014), 568–590. DOI: 10.3390/en7020568.

**Bibliographic verification:** Publisher full-text page confirms title, authors, journal, volume, issue, pages, publication date and DOI. Full article text inspected.

**System / study type:** Primary experimental study of alkaline sodium-silicate gels designed for in-depth reservoir profile modification. Commercial sodium silicate (molar SiO2:Na2O ratio 3.35; stock solution 35.7 wt%) was diluted to 3–6 wt% silicate content. HCl was used to adjust pH, typically in the alkaline range pH 8.6–10.7. Temperature, silicate concentration, pH, shear history and divalent ions (Ca2+, Mg2+) were investigated. This is a waterglass gelation system, not acid-precipitated industrial silica powder.

**Syneresis method:** Samples were kept in sealed polypropylene containers to prevent evaporation. Expelled liquid appearing at the surface was removed and weighed over time. The authors converted expelled-liquid mass to wt% relative to initial sample mass, assuming initial solution and expelled liquid had approximately equal densities. Therefore, this is a direct gravimetric liquid-expulsion measurement, but not a direct measurement of three-dimensional gel-volume shrinkage.

**Directly reported findings:**
- For a given pH, higher silicate content advances the onset of shrinkage and increases the shrinkage rate and total expelled-liquid fraction.
- At pH 10.10 and 20 °C, a 6 wt% sodium-silicate gel expels approximately 25 wt% liquid by 400 h, compared with approximately 8 wt% for a 4 wt% gel. The initial rates are reported as approximately 0.1 and 0.025 wt% per hour, respectively.
- At a fixed silicate content, lower pH advances syneresis and increases the rate.
- For 4.5 wt% silicate at pH 10.30, the reported shrinkage rates rise from approximately 0.013 wt%/h at 20 °C to 0.054 wt%/h at 50 °C and 0.21 wt%/h at 80 °C.
- Adding 500 ppm CaCl2 to the 4.5 wt%, pH 10.30 system increases the reported shrinkage by about a factor of three relative to the no-CaCl2 case. At the compared 0.009 M divalent-ion condition, Mg2+ produces less shrinkage than Ca2+.
- Imposed pressure accelerates liquid expulsion: at 50 bar, the rate after the maximum-strength time is reported above 1 wt%/h, compared with less than 0.1 wt%/h under ambient conditions. The gel remains intact over the tested pressure range.
- Container material has a striking effect. For 6 wt% silicate at pH 10.10, about 2.3 wt% shrinkage is reported after one week in glass versus about 12 wt% in polypropylene. The authors attribute this to possible silicate adsorption/chemisorption at the glass surface and different gel-wall adhesion.

**What this supports:** In alkaline waterglass gels, syneresis/liquid expulsion is coupled to the evolving gel strength and is sensitive to silicate concentration, pH, temperature, divalent ions, pressure and wall boundary conditions. The strong wall-material effect is particularly important: measured shrinkage is not solely an intrinsic bulk-network property.

**What it does not establish:** It does not establish the same quantitative dependencies in industrial sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid precipitated silica. The gels are much more dilute (3–6 wt% sodium silicate), strongly alkaline, acidified with HCl, and intended for porous-reservoir applications. It does not measure primary-particle size, aggregate structure, BET area or final powder properties. Gravimetric liquid loss is used as a shrinkage proxy under an equal-density assumption; it should not be pooled directly with geometric ΔV/V0 data from Wilhelm & Kind.

**Methodological limitations / transferability:** The sealed-container setup limits evaporation, but container-wall chemistry and adhesion materially change the result. The authors note that the gel separates from polypropylene walls but remains attached to glass, producing different shrinkage geometries. Pressure-driven liquid expulsion is an imposed-load test and not equivalent to natural syneresis. The system includes divalent ions that can precipitate metal silicates; in some conditions, precipitation and gelation overlap. Report the exact formulation and container material whenever citing numerical values.

**Review role:** Independent mechanistic comparator for alkaline waterglass gelation and syneresis; especially useful for showing the importance of boundary conditions and chemical composition. Do not use as direct evidence for industrial precipitated silica. **Provisional class B; OBS+CALC.**

## Synthesis note

PSG-032 strengthens the case that syneresis cannot be interpreted from temperature or pH alone: composition, ionic environment, gel strength and wall/drainage boundary conditions also matter. Its gravimetric endpoint differs from the geometric volume-shrinkage measurements used in the KIT precipitated-silica studies. Keep the datasets in separate comparison strata.
