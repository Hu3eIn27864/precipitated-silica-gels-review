## PSG-018 — Quarch (2010), doctoral dissertation

**Verified reference:** Kerstin Quarch, *Produktgestaltung an kolloidalen Agglomeraten und Gelen: Gelierung und Fragmentierung anorganisch gefällten Siliciumdioxids*. Dissertation, Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, 2010. KIT Scientific Publishing, 204 pp., ISBN 978-3-86644-503-1. DOI: 10.5445/KSP/1000017100.

**Verification:** The KIT library catalogue identifies this as a 2010 dissertation and online book. The full institutional PDF was inspected. The thesis states that the research was conducted at the Institute of Thermal Process Engineering from March 2005 to October 2009 and defended on 16 March 2010.

**Study type and material:** Original experimental programme on inorganic precipitated silica, using sodium silicate and sulfuric acid. It covers semi-batch precipitation, rheology of quiescently gelled material, batch precipitation, stirred-tank and Taylor–Couette fragmentation, ultrasound dispersion, ageing, and freeze-drying.

### A. Gelation and rheology

The thesis defines the gel point operationally by the crossover of storage and loss moduli, (G'=G''). Both moduli increase during gelation; the thesis interprets this as increasing effective solid volume and interparticle network formation. It also notes that post-gel polycondensation continues and the moduli continue to rise, i.e. the gel becomes mechanically stronger after the nominal gel point.

For the illustrated rheology condition (0.066 g sodium silicate per g water and 0.017 g sulfuric acid per g water), gelation occurs within about 90 s. The author varies acid loading/pH, ionic strength (Na₂SO₄ addition), temperature and silicate concentration.

A central result is that acid- and base-catalysed gels do not lie on one common normalised rheological master curve. At equal solids content and gelation time, acid-catalysed gels are stronger; the reported asymptotic elastic modulus is about one order of magnitude higher than for basic gels. The thesis relates this to structural differences and ionic strength, not simply primary-particle size.

**Review interpretation:** This is strong evidence that pH/chemistry changes gel network structure and strength. It supports a process-history-to-fragmentability link. Avoid reducing the effect to “pH alone”: acid loading, silicate concentration, ionic strength and gelation kinetics covary in parts of the experimental matrix.

### B. Fragmentation and composition

The batch studies show that faster gelation generally produces larger fragments in the tested composition range. The maximum fragment size, approximated by (x_{90,3}), is reported between about 120 and 240 µm. Acidic fragments are initially smaller in some comparisons but more resistant to further ultrasonic fragmentation; the thesis interprets this as a denser or more strongly connected internal structure, with open outer regions breaking first and denser cores resisting later breakup.

This is not a universal pH ranking: the thesis notes that the response depends on the combination of silicate loading, acid loading, solids content and gelation time. In some basic experiments the starting aggregate size appears more important than gelation time.

**Review interpretation:** Fragment size is not controlled by mechanical energy input alone. The evolving gel strength and structure inherited from the precipitation/gelation stage are co-determinants. A concise synthesis should distinguish (i) maximum fragment size in the reactor and (ii) subsequent resistance to secondary breakup during ultrasound treatment.

### C. Reactor hydrodynamics and energy-input heterogeneity

The thesis compares stirred-tank and Taylor–Couette operation. Bimodal fragment-size distributions are observed even in the Taylor–Couette reactor, where energy input is more homogeneous. Increasing rotational speed shifts distributions toward smaller fragments, but the distribution shape is not eliminated or made monomodal simply by changing reactor type. The thesis concludes that energy-input magnitude has a significant influence; the reactor type only modestly affects distribution form in the tested cases.

The thesis also reports that the largest aggregate size can be controlled through energy input only during the first minutes after gelation. It does not observe a sustained dynamic equilibrium between breakage and reagglomeration. The proposed explanation is the formation of solid bridges between primary particles as residual monomer continues to condense; once these bridges form, much higher energy is required to break the aggregates and collision-induced sticking becomes unlikely.

**Review interpretation:** This supports a time-dependent “fragmentability window” after gelation. It is an important nuance for scale-up: specific energy alone is incomplete unless its timing relative to gel age and network strengthening is specified.

### D. Ageing and early syneresis evidence

The thesis includes preliminary ageing tests on a basic gel. After gel removal at 45 min (gel point around 35 min), the particle-size distribution shifts toward smaller sizes after one day, while the ultrasound dispersion curve becomes flatter, indicating stronger gel fragments. After about one week, no further differences were detected in those measures.

However, the author reports that macroscopic shrinkage was too small for reliable quantification under the chosen conditions. BET surface-area values of fresh gels varied widely (about 50–250 m²/g), probably because incomplete condensation and drying artefacts affected the measurements. The thesis explicitly treats these as preliminary observations.

**Review interpretation:** The ageing results support continuing structural strengthening, but they do not provide a robust quantitative syneresis law. Do not use these preliminary BET/shrinkage observations as definitive evidence for changes in final surface area or porosity.

### E. Methodological limitations

1. **Dynamic light scattering:** the thesis itself warns that the intensity-weighted (z)-average is strictly comparable with other measurements only for monodisperse spherical particles; software-derived distributions are assumption-dependent; increasing suspension viscosity approaches the method's limits because it assumes free particle diffusion.
2. **Ultrasound dispersion:** useful as an operational measure of fragment resistance, but not equivalent to in-reactor hydrodynamic fragmentation. Energy dose and acoustic field must be specified.
3. **BET after drying:** fresh-gel results show large scatter and possible drying artefacts; these data cannot safely establish wet-state internal surface evolution.
4. **Mechanistic statements:** several explanations are explicitly hypotheses (e.g. solid-bridge formation and the highly viscous droplet analogy); preserve that epistemic status.
5. **Transferability:** the work uses laboratory reactors and model recipes. Direct industrial scale-up needs reactor-specific hydrodynamics, residence-time/gel-age distribution and energy-dissipation characterisation.

**Overall appraisal:** **Class A** for direct industrially relevant precipitated-silica gelation and fragmentation experiments. The dissertation is a major source for the review, but its chapters overlap with the Quarch & Kind 2010 Part 1/Part 2 papers and Quarch et al. 2010 *Chemical Engineering Research and Design* paper. Treat these as a single research family until experiment-by-experiment overlap is mapped.

## New synthesis points to carry into the review

1. **Gel point is not a terminal state.** Rheological strengthening continues after (G'=G''), consistent with ongoing polycondensation and network development.
2. **Acidic and basic gels differ structurally and mechanically.** At comparable solids content and gelation time, acidic gels are reported to be substantially stronger; the thesis attributes this to gel structure and ionic-strength effects, not only primary particle size.
3. **There is a post-gel fragmentation window.** The ability to control maximum aggregate size by energy input is strongest in the first minutes after gelation; subsequent solid-bridge formation is proposed to increase resistance to breakup.
4. **A more homogeneous energy field does not guarantee a monomodal size distribution.** Taylor–Couette tests still show bimodality; energy-input magnitude and evolving gel structure matter.
5. **Ageing changes fragment strength, but early BET/shrinkage data are weak.** This is a promising mechanistic link, not a robust quantitative endpoint in the thesis.
