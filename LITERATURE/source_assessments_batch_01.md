## PSG-001 — Schlomach & Kind (2004)

**Reference:** J. Schlomach and M. Kind, “Investigations on the semi-batch precipitation of silica,” *Journal of Colloid and Interface Science* 277(2) (2004), 316–326. DOI: 10.1016/j.jcis.2004.04.051.

**Verification:** PubMed and ScienceDirect records agree on title, authors, date, volume, issue, pages and DOI. Abstract inspected; publisher full text is access-restricted in the available record.

**System / study type:** Semi-batch precipitation from sodium silicate and sulfuric acid; primary experimental process study with kinetic analysis.

**Directly reported:** The process is resolved into primary-particle production, aggregation up to the gel point, and post-gel fragmentation/compaction. Aggregation progress was analysed by photon-correlation spectroscopy. The reported reaction-limited aggregation kernel has a time-dependent prefactor (B'(t)) of approximately (3.4\times10^{-20}) to (2.6\times10^{-20}\;\mathrm{L/min}), weakly dependent on time. The reported gel-cluster fractal dimension rises from approximately 1.8 to approximately 2.4 after the gel point and then remains roughly constant during the reported interval.

**What this supports:** The precipitation pathway contains distinguishable stages, and the post-gel stage is not simply “more aggregation”: mechanical fragmentation and compaction are part of the process description. It is a central source for the process-history framing.

**What it does not establish:** A universal fractal dimension or universal kinetic kernel for all precipitated silica formulations, scales or mixing geometries. The abstract alone does not establish the sensitivity of the inferred fractal dimension to the photon-correlation inversion/model or the full measurement window.

**Critical appraisal / extraction needs:** Obtain full text to record feed concentrations, addition and mixing conditions, sampling protocol, photon-correlation analysis assumptions, uncertainty and length/time windows. Cross-check the same experiment/dataset against Schlomach’s 2006 dissertation and Sahabi & Kind’s later use of the post-gel fractal-dimension trajectory; these are not independent replications unless independently prepared and measured.

**Review role:** Core evidence for precipitation → aggregation → gel point → fragmentation/compaction. **Class A; OBS+CALC.**

## PSG-002 — Quarch & Kind (2010), Part 1: gelation kinetics and gel properties

**Reference:** K. Quarch and M. Kind, “Inorganic Precipitated Silica Gel. Part 1: Gelation Kinetics and Gel Properties,” *Chemical Engineering & Technology* 33(6) (2010), 1034–1039. DOI: 10.1002/ceat.201000080.

**Verification:** KITopen and Wiley records agree on authors, journal, year, volume, issue, pages and DOI. Wiley abstract inspected.

**System / study type:** Industrially relevant silica precipitation by mixing silicate solution and acid in a semi-batch process; primary experimental communication.

**Directly reported:** Polycondensation of monomeric silica forms particles which aggregate to a particulate gel. In the stirred process the gel is immediately fragmented by stirrer energy; subsequent shrinkage and compaction of fragments form product aggregates. Part 1 investigates unstirred gels and the influence of temperature, ionic strength and reactant composition (which affects pH) on gel properties, particularly elasticity and hence fragmentation behaviour. The abstract states that gelation time can be qualitatively estimated from feed-material concentration.

**What this supports:** Gel mechanical properties at the point of fragmentation depend on the chemical and thermal history of gelation. This creates a plausible and important link between upstream precipitation conditions and downstream fragment size.

**What it does not establish:** A complete quantitative law connecting feed concentration to gelation time, nor a universal fragment-size correlation. The accessible abstract does not provide the actual formulation matrix, elasticity method, uncertainties or parameter ranges.

**Critical appraisal / extraction needs:** Full-text extraction is required for exact feed concentrations, pH/ionic-strength calculation, temperature range, elasticity measurement and the basis/limits of the gel-time estimate. Compare with the related dissertation by Quarch (2010); identify reused experiments.

**Review role:** Core bridge from precipitation chemistry to gel mechanics and fragmentability. **Class A; OBS.**

## PSG-016 — Quarch & Kind (2010), Part 2: fragmentation by mechanical energy

**Reference:** K. Quarch and M. Kind, “Inorganic Precipitated Silica Gel. Part 2: Fragmentation by Mechanical Energy,” *Chemical Engineering & Technology* 33(7) (2010), 1208–1212. DOI: 10.1002/ceat.201000081.

**Verification:** KIT TVT publication list confirms title, authors, journal, volume/pages and year; an indexed bibliographic record confirms the DOI pairing. Publisher-level full text not yet inspected.

**System / study type:** Follow-on primary study on fragmentation of inorganic precipitated silica gel under mechanical loading.

**Directly reported from available abstract/metadata:** The process context is sodium-silicate/acid precipitation; gel is fragmented by stirrer-derived mechanical energy, and fragment shrinkage/compaction contributes to final aggregates.

**Critical appraisal:** Do not infer detailed breakage laws or numerical results from the short abstract. Full text is needed for the apparatus, energy-dissipation measure, gel-age dependence, fragment-size distributions and model fits.

**Review role:** Core source for the gel-fragmentation step, complementary to Part 1. **Provisional A; OBS; full-text extraction pending.**

The two publications must have separate database records.

## PSG-017 — Quarch et al. (2010), mechanical fragmentation of precipitated silica aggregates

**Reference:** K. Quarch, E. Durand, C. Schilde, A. Kwade and M. Kind, “Mechanical fragmentation of precipitated silica aggregates,” *Chemical Engineering Research and Design* 88(12) (2010), 1639–1647. DOI: 10.1016/j.cherd.2010.01.007.

**Verification:** ScienceDirect publisher record and KIT TVT publication list confirm title, author list, journal, volume, issue, pages and DOI. Publisher abstract and article preview inspected.

**System / study type:** Primary experimental study. Sodium silicate and sulfuric acid are rapidly mixed in a Y-mixer before exposure to mechanical stress; fragmentation is investigated in a continuously stirred tank, a Taylor–Couette reactor and by ultrasound dispersion.

**Directly reported:** Fragment size depends on mechanical energy input and gel mechanical properties. The latter vary with gel age, solids content, pH, salt content and temperature. The authors report that, for the studied breakage mechanics, the gel behaves like a highly viscous liquid. The article also reports bimodal particle-size distributions from the stirred tank and discusses the possible role of power-distribution heterogeneity; material bridges between particles are identified as important.

**What this supports:** Fragmentation is controlled jointly by hydrodynamic/mechanical forcing and the evolving mechanical state of the gel. Reactor type and energy-input distribution matter; “stirring speed” alone is not a sufficient process descriptor.

**What it does not establish:** A universal scale-up law for all industrial reactors. A breakage analogy to highly viscous liquids should be presented as a result/interpretation for the tested conditions, not a universal constitutive identity.

**Critical appraisal / extraction needs:** Extract exact energy-dissipation definitions, reactor geometries, residence/age distributions, solids and salt ranges, particle-size measurement technique, ultrasound energy, and statistical distribution metrics. Distinguish the effects of gel strength from hydrodynamic stress distribution.

**Review role:** Key primary evidence for the fragmentation step and process-to-aggregate-size pathway. **Class A; OBS+CALC.**

## PSG-003 — Sahabi & Kind (2011), oedometer consolidation

**Reference:** H. Sahabi and M. Kind, “Consolidation of Inorganic Precipitated Silica Gel,” *Polymers* 3(3) (2011), 1423–1432. DOI: 10.3390/polym3031423.

**Verification:** Publisher full text inspected.

**System / study type:** Primary experimental study of precipitated silica gels made by rapid Y-nozzle mixing of sodium silicate and sulfuric acid. Acidic and alkaline model gels were prepared with short gelation times (~12 min): acid gel from 50 wt% sulfuric acid and 40 wt% sodium silicate solution at 1:1 ratio (reported pH −0.97); alkaline gel from 3.2 wt% sulfuric acid and 40 wt% sodium silicate at 1:1 (reported pH ~9).

**Methods:** One-dimensional oedometer compression using a universal testing machine, filter plates and nanofiltration membranes to retain gel fragments while pore liquid drains. The study varied temperature and compared acidic and alkaline gels. It used a Terzaghi/Shirato-style semi-empirical consolidation description.

**Directly reported:** Under an applied pressure of approximately 15 bar, the gel response is strongly time-dependent. For the illustrated conditions, about 50% shrinkage is reached around four times faster for alkaline gel and around five times faster for acidic gel at 80 °C than at 20 °C. The acidic gel consolidates faster than the alkaline gel in the reported comparison. The authors compare this compression response with natural ageing shrinkage: natural syneresis was approximately 15% over 70–150 h in cited acidic samples, whereas the oedometer example reached approximately 75% in about 20 h.

**Critical interpretation:** The oedometer is an accelerated, mechanically loaded drainage/compression experiment. It is not a direct measurement of unforced natural syneresis. The pressure boundary condition can destroy/rearrange microscale structure; the authors explicitly suggest microscale structure is initially destroyed during testing. The Terzaghi-type model does not capture all primary and secondary consolidation behaviour; the paper notes deviations and the need for a damping element.

**What it supports:** Quantitative comparison of gel response under controlled compression; strong dependence on temperature and pH; utility of oedometer testing as a faster characterisation tool.

**What it does not establish:** That 75% compression shrinkage represents the extent of natural syneresis or that the Terzaghi model identifies the microscopic consolidation mechanism.

**Review role:** Core evidence for mechanically enforced consolidation and for the methodological distinction between natural ageing and compression tests. **Class A; OBS+CALC.**

## PSG-004 — Sahabi & Kind (2011), model-like description of consolidation

**Reference:** H. Sahabi and M. Kind, “Experimentally Justified Model-Like Description of Consolidation of Precipitated Silica,” *Polymers* 3(4) (2011), 2156–2171. DOI: 10.3390/polym3042156.

**Verification:** Full KIT-hosted article PDF inspected.

**System / study type:** Molecular-dynamics modelling of a precipitated-silica gel fragment and a larger particulate network, compared with previous experimental and Monte Carlo data.

**Model details:** LAMMPS MD simulations; a model gel fragment consists of 1,000 primary particles. The particle interaction is represented using integrated Lennard-Jones terms for van der Waals attraction and short-range Pauli/Born repulsion; solvent particles are represented explicitly in a lattice-like environment. An electrostatic repulsive term is also tested.

**Directly reported:** The MD fractal-dimension trajectory agrees less well with the experimental data than the earlier Monte Carlo simulation, with deviations between the beginning and end. Agreement requires an empirical time-step scaling of 100 (one minute mapped to 100 simulation steps). For the larger network/oedometer comparison, a time scaling factor of 10 is used. The authors state that fluid flow through the particle network is omitted and is needed for a more complete temporal description. They also report that adding DLVO electrostatic repulsion does not reproduce the observed consolidation in their model.

**What it supports:** Van der Waals attraction and thermal motion are plausible candidate drivers for particle rearrangement/densification in the modelled system. It demonstrates a route to connecting particle-scale interaction potentials with a macroscopic consolidation curve.

**What it does not establish:** Direct proof that van der Waals forces dominate every real precipitated-silica gel or that the simulated timescale is physically predictive. The required time rescaling, idealised initial aggregate, simplified solvent/hydrodynamic treatment and omission of pore-fluid flow are material limitations.

**Review role:** Mechanistic model with explicit caveats; useful in a “what can be inferred from simulations?” subsection, not as stand-alone experimental proof. **Class A for system relevance; evidence tag MODEL+SIM, moderate mechanistic confidence.**

## PSG-005 — Wilhelm & Kind (2014), natural versus enforced syneresis

**Reference:** S. Wilhelm and M. Kind, “On the Relation between Natural and Enforced Syneresis of Acidic Precipitated Silica,” *Polymers* 6(12) (2014), 2896–2911. DOI: 10.3390/polym6122896.

**Verification:** KITopen record and publisher full-text page identified; the full accessible supplementary/main article text was inspected for the methods and correlation limits.

**System / study type:** Acidic sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid precipitated silica gel. Natural syneresis is compared with compression-driven “enforced syneresis” using a plunger/pressure cell.

**Methods and conditions visible in the full text:** Y-mixer at 1:1 volumetric ratio; sodium water glass at 40 wt% solution; sulfuric acid at 50 wt%; gelation time around 12 min at 20 °C; theoretical pH reported as −0.97. Natural shrinkage is measured using an adapted pycnometer. Enforced shrinkage is measured in a pressure cell under an external load.

**Directly reported:** A correlative model is developed to infer natural syneresis from enforced syneresis by extrapolating fitted shrinkage and characteristic-time parameters toward zero applied pressure. For the acid system, the predicted rate agrees reasonably with measurements, but maximum shrinkage prediction has relative errors of approximately 25% and 45% at 20 and 60 °C. The authors explicitly state that more data at low pressure differences are needed to improve the extrapolation.

**Critical interpretation:** The method is an empirical acceleration/correlation strategy, not proof that natural and enforced syneresis have identical microscopic mechanisms. The low-pressure extrapolation is particularly sensitive; accuracy differs by response variable and temperature.

**Review role:** Core methodological source for accelerated syneresis testing, with strong emphasis on calibration domain and extrapolation uncertainty. **Class A; OBS+CALC+MODEL.**

## PSG-006 — Wilhelm & Kind (2015), pH, temperature and sample size

**Reference:** S. Wilhelm and M. Kind, “Influence of pH, Temperature and Sample Size on Natural and Enforced Syneresis of Precipitated Silica,” *Polymers* 7(12) (2015), 2504–2521. DOI: 10.3390/polym7121528.

**Verification:** Publisher full text and KITopen record inspected.

**System / study type:** Direct experiments on sodium-silicate/sulfuric-acid precipitated silica, with acid- and base-catalysed model gels. This is a detailed parameter study, not a general review.

**Preparation and methods:** Y-mixer at 1:1 volumetric ratio; sodium silicate solution at 40 wt% (based on water glass specified as 35.5 wt% Na₂O·3.3SiO₂); sulfuric acid solution at 50 wt% for acid-catalysed gel (pH < 0) and 3.2 wt% for base-catalysed gel (pH ≈ 10). Natural syneresis was measured by pycnometry for cylindrical samples of 12 mm diameter and ~3 mL volume; three samples were averaged per measurement point. Small droplets (~3 mm) were measured optically. Enforced syneresis used a uniaxial testing machine and a pressure cell.

**Directly reported:** For the cylindrical acid-catalysed samples, natural maximum shrinkage is approximately 0.20 across 20, 40 and 60 °C, while the characteristic time decreases from about 21.3 h at 20 °C to 5.0 h at 60 °C. For the base-catalysed samples, maximum shrinkage increases from about 0.30 to 0.51 across the same temperatures, while the time constant decreases from about 50.1 h to 27.5 h. Smaller gel samples shrink faster; their maximum shrinkage can also differ from that of larger cylinders. The enforced-syneresis model predicts acid-gel natural maximum shrinkage in the range 0.19–0.30 compared with measured ~0.20, but the base-gel extrapolation can overestimate natural shrinkage (reported prediction range ~0.52–0.75 versus measured ~0.30–0.51).

**Critical interpretation:** The abstract’s statement that dependencies are “analogous” should not be overread as quantitative equivalence. The full results show that the correlative model is more reliable for some cases/observables than others, and the base-catalysed extrapolation can overpredict. Sample size changes drainage length and rate; the 3 mm droplet and 12 mm cylinder are not interchangeable geometries. The study itself notes that ionic strength and silicate concentration were not varied in this paper.

**Review role:** Core evidence that pH, temperature and geometry jointly control syneresis, and that accelerated test correlations have bounded validity. **Class A; OBS+CALC+MODEL.**

## Cross-source synthesis and unresolved checks

1. **The process chain is directly supported, but the transitions are not all equally quantified.** Schlomach & Kind (2004) resolve aggregation/gel-point/post-gel stages; Quarch et al. (2010) show that gel age and formulation influence mechanical fragmentation; Sahabi & Kind quantify post-fragment compression/consolidation; Wilhelm & Kind quantify unforced and forced syneresis in model systems.
2. **Gelation chemistry sets mechanical state.** Quarch Part 1 and the fragmentation paper support a link from pH/composition/temperature/ionic strength and gel age to gel elasticity/strength and fragment size. This is more defensible than asserting a single universal causal parameter.
3. **Fragmentation and consolidation must remain separate.** Fragmentation is stress-induced breakup of a gel network; consolidation/syneresis is subsequent densification and liquid expulsion. In stirred systems they can overlap in time, making attribution difficult.
4. **Compression tests are not natural syneresis.** Oedometer/enforced-syneresis methods accelerate drainage but alter boundary conditions and may disrupt microstructure.
5. **The interaction mechanism remains partly model-based.** Van der Waals-driven rearrangement is plausible in Sahabi & Kind’s model, but fluid flow is omitted and the simulated timescale is rescaled.
6. **Avoid treating related papers as independent replications.** The Quarch/Kind and Sahabi/Kind/Wilhelm/Kind publications share an institutional research programme and may reuse recipes, apparatus or underlying experimental datasets. Build a study-family map before counting independent evidence.

### Supplementary extraction note — geometry dependence in the 2015 study (11 Nov 2026)

A closer re-read of the full publisher text makes the geometry effect more specific:

- **Cylinders:** diameter 12 mm, gel volume approximately 3 mL; natural syneresis measured by adapted pycnometry, with three samples averaged per point. For acid-catalysed gel, maximum relative shrinkage is about 0.20 at 20–60 °C, while the fitted characteristic time falls from about 21.3 h to 5.0 h. For base-catalysed gel, maximum shrinkage rises from about 0.30 to 0.51 and the time constant falls from about 50.1 h to 27.5 h.
- **Droplets:** diameter approximately 3 mm; shrinkage measured optically. The reported fitted maximum shrinkage for acid-catalysed droplets rises from 0.37 (20 °C) to 0.48 (60 °C), while the corresponding base-catalysed values rise from 0.30 to 0.51. The tabulated characteristic times are sub-hour (approximately 0.40–0.95 h, depending on pH and temperature).
- The droplet protocol holds samples at 20 °C during the initial period after gelation and then changes to the target temperature. The authors themselves note that this heating transient may explain similarities between the 40 and 60 °C droplet curves early in the experiment.

**Interpretive consequence:** “Sample size” is not merely a geometric correction factor. It changes the drainage length and apparent consolidation kinetics, and may change the measured maximum shrinkage. The different thermal histories of the cylinder and droplet protocols also mean that geometry effects should not be interpreted as a perfectly isolated one-variable comparison. Natural-syneresis datasets should therefore be compared only with geometry, exposed surface/drainage boundary, sample age and temperature history reported alongside the shrinkage metric.

**Important boundary:** The authors describe natural and enforced syneresis as having analogous parameter dependencies, but this does not mean their absolute shrinkage, kinetics or microscopic pathways are interchangeable. The forced test imposes a mechanical pressure gradient and uses a membrane; the natural test does not.
